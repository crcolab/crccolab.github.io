Why "cutting it all off" isn't so easy

Taiwan's National Security Bureau says that in 2024, government agencies logged an average of

2.4 million

cyberattacks a day

Daily average in 2023: 1.2 million

National Security Bureau (Taiwan)

  • To attack Taiwan, spy on it, or run foreign information manipulation and interference against it, an adversary first needs a connection to Taiwan's internet
  • That connection is shared: cut all of it, and the adversary's own people on the inside lose it too
  • Cutting it once isn't hard. Keeping it completely cut is

A few days of on-and-off service is more likely than the whole internet going dark.

An internet outage may look a lot like rolling blackouts

  1. NormalMonday morning
  2. Slow, endless spinning+2 hours
  3. Down+1 hour
  4. Some services backNext day
  5. Down againDay 3
  6. Back onlineDay 5

Power works the same way. Taiwan doesn't lose power all at once; outages roll by area, crews make repairs, and the power comes back. Internet repairs are done by people too: cable ships, backup routes, detours, and throttling.

So what you have to get through is the back and forth. If everything goes down at once, you probably have worse problems to worry about

What people in Taiwan worry about in an emergency…

  1. 1

    When power and internet both vanish for a long time, a more direct danger usually comes with it

  2. 2

    That's what your emergency supply kit and evacuation plan are for

  3. 3

    Getting "I'm OK" out to people

  4. 4

    Opening your work files so your organization keeps running

If things really reach items 1 and 2, what you need more is a physical go-bag. Most digital resilience scenarios stop at items 3 and 4

If the international links go down, does the island still connect?

  • Probably still worksCalling family and coworkers: calls and texts run on Taiwan's own telecom networks, and the switching happens on the island
  • Probably still worksOffice Wi-Fi and your company's internal file servers: those connections never leave the country
  • May be partly down"Taiwanese" websites and apps: it depends on whether their logins, payments, and CDNs are overseas (Part 1)
  • Very likely downFiles kept only in an overseas cloud, with no copy anywhere else

In theory, the hardest part is getting your important files out of overseas clouds and into your own hands first.

What about LoRa mesh (e.g., Meshtastic)?

What it can do

  • Plain-text messages of a few hundred bytes
  • No cell towers or carriers needed
  • License-free bands; with a clear line of sight, it reaches from a hundred meters to a few kilometers
  • Fun to play with, and it mostly works

What it can't do

  • Reliably send files, photos, voice, or video
  • Guaranteed, instant delivery like LINE, the messaging app most people in Taiwan use
  • "Install the app and you'll find your family"
  • Island-wide coverage (no one is responsible for building that network)

It's definitely not "buy one of these and you'll stay connected in an outage." You can use it, but know that you're buying a technology that's still early in development.

What you'd have to solve to rely on LoRa mesh

  • If the two ends can't reach each other, then what?
  • Where do the relay nodes go? Whose rooftop?
  • Who pays for the power to run that relay?
  • After the power goes out, how long can the relay keep running?
  • Will relays that other hobbyists set up be taken down someday?
  • Who fixes broken gear? Who handles firmware updates?
  • When the settings drift six months later, who fixes them?
  • Someone has to retest every so often. When a test fails, who adds nodes?

LoRa mesh is definitely not a backup channel that's guaranteed to get through.

In practice, you're building your own small radio network.

So you need a digital go-bag

From why to prepare to what to pack: the go-bag, backups, a framework, and the four-part map.

A digital go-bag works like an emergency supply kit

Emergency supply kit

When the water or the power goes out, you won't have to rush out and fight over supplies

  • Three days of water and nonperishable food
  • Flashlight, batteries, and a radio
  • Everyday medicines and a list of your prescriptions
  • Some cash

Digital go-bag

When the internet goes down, you won't have to go asking for help right away

  • Work files that open offline
  • Contact lists, rosters, and address books
  • Passwords and 2FA backup codes
  • A plan on paper (your SOP)

If everyone can get through their own first three days, society and the economy have a much smaller shock to absorb all at once.

A go-bag isn't only for you. It keeps rescue capacity and scarce bandwidth free for the people who need them most.

Daily life and business have to go on

Staying in touch matters. But if the connection comes back and you still can't get to your work files, it doesn't do you much good. Everyone still has to work, and work runs on files. Even at war, Ukraine keeps working

When the internet is shaky, backups matter more

  1. And offices already have the answer: every office network is a local network (LAN) of your own
  2. When the internet goes down, nobody has to haul an antenna up a mountain. Fall back to your local network
